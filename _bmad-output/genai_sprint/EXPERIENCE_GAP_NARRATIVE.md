**Q: "What's the biggest thing you've been wrong about?"**

## THREE THINGS NEVER TO DO

1. **Never inflate.** Not by rounding, not by counting college projects, not by letting an ambiguous phrasing sit. If asked directly, answer directly. Beyond the ethics, TCS runs background verification against your ElasticRun records — a number that doesn't match kills an offer *after* you've resigned elsewhere.
2. **Never lead with the gap.** Do not open a round with "I know I have less experience than…". Let them raise it. If they don't raise it, it isn't the deciding factor and you'd be manufacturing an objection.
3. **Never over-argue it.** Give the answer once, well, and move to the work. Repeating it signals that *you* think it's disqualifying. The frame you want in the room is: this is a known fact, it's been addressed, next question.

# THE COMPOUND QUESTION — added 28 Jul 2026

Two facts are now in the room: you are **under the stated band**, and your last role **ended following a PIP**. An interviewer may connect them explicitly. This section is only for when they do.

## The rules

1. **Never pre-empt the connection.** Do not say "I know it looks bad that I'm under the band *and* I left under a cloud." You would be manufacturing the strongest objection against yourself, and interviewers frequently never make the link at all.
2. **Answer them separately when asked separately.** Band question → band answer. Separation question → `SEPARATION_NARRATIVE.md` §1 or §2. Do not let one answer bleed into the other.
3. **When they connect them, answer the connection — do not re-argue either half.** Re-running the tenure argument at this point sounds like a man producing a second defence for the same charge.

## ⭐ The answer, if they connect them

**Q: "You're under the band, and your last role ended under a performance plan. Why would we take that risk?"**

> "That's the fair version of the question and I'd rather you asked it. Two separate things, and I'd answer them separately.
>
> On the band — I'd say test it in the next hour rather than take my word. Six hundred tracked items across three product domains in two years, a framework other teams built on, a permission model on a security system. If that doesn't hold up under technical questioning, that's a real answer and I'll take it.
>
> On the separation — it was a specific mismatch in a specific role: «THE TRUE THING». I own it, and the fix is a working habit rather than an intention. What I'd say about the two together is that they're not the same risk. One is about whether I can do the work. The other is about whether I read priorities right in one context. You can test the first today, and the second is cheaper to test in sixty days than to argue about now."

**Three beats, then stop.** Concede the question is fair, split the two, offer a test for each. Do not add a fourth beat, do not reassure, do not thank them for their understanding.

## What NOT to say — these collapse under verification

- **"The PIP was really about the reorg / the domain change, not performance."** Even if partly true, it relocates cause and it is unfalsifiable. It also directly contradicts the ownership posture in the answer above.
- **"My performance reviews were actually strong."** If it were true, you would not have been on a PIP, and TCS's BGV can ask ElasticRun for the reason for separation. Do not put a claim into the room that a form can contradict.
- **"I was carrying more than my grade, which is why."** This tries to make the band gap explain the PIP away. It converts two separate risks into one narrative that sounds rehearsed and that no document supports.

**The honest alternative to all three is the answer above:** own the specific feedback, name the specific fix, and offer a specific test. That version survives a background check, because there is nothing in it for a background check to contradict.

## One thing that genuinely helps, and is true

The strongest evidence against "this person under-performs" is **not** an argument — it is the thing you built while unemployed with nobody managing you. `SEPARATION_NARRATIVE.md` §6 covers how to put it on the table rather than describe it. Someone who shipped an evaluated system in a few weeks with no manager, no deadline and no salary is making an empirical case, not a rhetorical one.
